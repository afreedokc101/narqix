## Why this matters

You do not need to pretend to be American. You do need to understand the person on the other end: what time it is where they are, what their address means, what "I'm good" really means, and why nobody answers on Thanksgiving. This page gives you that context so you sound informed and respectful, never fake.

## 1. US time zones

The mainland US has four main time zones. Alaska and Hawaii have their own.

| Zone | Short name (winter / summer) | Example cities | Hours behind Dhaka (summer / winter) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Eastern | EST / EDT, written "ET" | New York, Atlanta, Miami, Boston, Detroit | 10 / 11 |
| Central | CST / CDT, written "CT" | Chicago, Dallas, Houston, Nashville, New Orleans | 11 / 12 |
| Mountain | MST / MDT, written "MT" | Denver, Salt Lake City, Albuquerque | 12 / 13 |
| Arizona (most of it) | MST all year, no clock change | Phoenix, Tucson | 13 all year |
| Pacific | PST / PDT, written "PT" | Los Angeles, San Diego, Seattle, Las Vegas, Portland | 13 / 14 |
| Alaska | AKST / AKDT | Anchorage | 14 / 15 |
| Hawaii | HST, no clock change | Honolulu | 16 all year |

**US clock changes:** most of the US moves clocks forward one hour on the **second Sunday of March** and back on the **first Sunday of November**. Hawaii and most of Arizona do not change. Bangladesh never changes. So your conversions shift twice a year. See Time Zones and Scheduling.

**States split between two time zones** (always check the city or county, not just the state):

- **Florida:** most is Eastern; the western panhandle (around Pensacola, Panama City) is Central.
- **Texas:** most is Central; El Paso and the far west are Mountain.
- **Tennessee:** east (Knoxville, Chattanooga) is Eastern; middle and west (Nashville, Memphis) are Central.
- **Kentucky:** east (Louisville, Lexington) is Eastern; west is Central.
- **Indiana:** mostly Eastern; northwest and southwest corners are Central.
- **Michigan:** almost all Eastern; a few western Upper Peninsula counties are Central.
- **Kansas, Nebraska, North Dakota, South Dakota:** mostly Central; western parts are Mountain.
- **Idaho:** south (Boise) is Mountain; the north panhandle is Pacific.
- **Oregon:** almost all Pacific; a small eastern area is Mountain.

<div class="callout callout-never">

**Never do this:** Never decide calling times from the state name alone. A Florida area code or a Texas address can be in a different zone than you expect, and the person may live somewhere else entirely. Follow the compliance rule: the time must be permitted in both the phone number's zone and the address zone.

</div>

## 2. States, codes and regions

The US has 50 states plus Washington, DC. Every state has a two-letter code used in addresses and the CRM. Learn the codes for the states your campaign calls most.

| Region | States (code) |
| --- | --- |
| **Northeast** | Connecticut (CT), Maine (ME), Massachusetts (MA), New Hampshire (NH), Rhode Island (RI), Vermont (VT), New Jersey (NJ), New York (NY), Pennsylvania (PA) |
| **Midwest** | Illinois (IL), Indiana (IN), Michigan (MI), Ohio (OH), Wisconsin (WI), Iowa (IA), Kansas (KS), Minnesota (MN), Missouri (MO), Nebraska (NE), North Dakota (ND), South Dakota (SD) |
| **South** | Delaware (DE), Florida (FL), Georgia (GA), Maryland (MD), North Carolina (NC), South Carolina (SC), Virginia (VA), Washington DC (DC), West Virginia (WV), Alabama (AL), Kentucky (KY), Mississippi (MS), Tennessee (TN), Arkansas (AR), Louisiana (LA), Oklahoma (OK), Texas (TX) |
| **West** | Arizona (AZ), Colorado (CO), Idaho (ID), Montana (MT), Nevada (NV), New Mexico (NM), Utah (UT), Wyoming (WY), Alaska (AK), California (CA), Hawaii (HI), Oregon (OR), Washington (WA) |

**Codes people often confuse:** MA (Massachusetts) vs MD (Maryland) vs ME (Maine) vs MI (Michigan) vs MN (Minnesota) vs MO (Missouri) vs MS (Mississippi) vs MT (Montana). AK is Alaska, not Arkansas (AR). AL is Alabama. IA is Iowa. Always check.

**Washington state vs Washington, DC:** "Washington" alone usually means the state in the far northwest (Seattle). "DC" or "DC area" means the capital on the east coast.

**Large states your campaigns often call:** Texas, Florida, California, Georgia, Ohio, North Carolina, Pennsylvania, Illinois, Michigan, Tennessee, Indiana, Arizona.

## 3. How US addresses work

**Standard format:**

> 1234 N Oak St, Apt 5B
Springfield, IL 62704
> 
- **House number, street direction, street name, street type**, then unit.
- **City, state code, ZIP code.**
- **ZIP code:** 5 digits. Sometimes followed by 4 more ("62704-1234", called ZIP+4).
- **ZIP codes can start with zero** (New England, New Jersey, Puerto Rico). Store them as text so the zero is not lost: 02134, not 2134.
- **PO Box:** a mailbox at a post office, not a physical home. A property cannot be at a PO Box.
- **Mailing address vs property address:** an owner may live in one place (mailing address) and own a property somewhere else. Never mix them up in the CRM.
- **County:** many property and legal records are kept by county, so the county name often matters in real estate and property work.
- **Rural addresses** may use "County Road", "Rural Route (RR)" or "Highway" numbers.

## 4. How Americans talk on the phone

**Greetings are not real questions.**

- "How are you?" or "How's it going?" is a greeting. Answer briefly and return it: "Good, thanks. How are you?" Do not give a long answer.

**"No" is often polite and indirect.** These usually mean "no, thank you":

- "I'm good." / "I'm all set." / "We're good."
- "I'll think about it." / "Maybe later." / "Let me talk to my wife and get back to you."
- "Not right now."

Listen for tone. "I'm good" with a rising, open tone may mean "I'm fine, go on". "I'm good" said quickly and flat almost always means "no thanks". When unsure, ask a soft, clear question: "Totally understand. Would it be OK if I checked back in a few months, or would you prefer I didn't?"

**Common phrases and what they mean:**

| They say | It means |
| --- | --- |
| "Hang on a sec." / "Give me a minute." | Please wait, I am coming back |
| "You bet." / "Sure thing." | Yes, happy to |
| "I'll let you go." | I want to end the call politely |
| "Have a good one." | Goodbye, have a nice day |
| "What's this regarding?" | Why are you calling? Be direct. |
| "Who is this?" | Say your name and company clearly, right away |
| "I'm not interested." | Respect it; follow your script's exit, and honour any stop request |
| "Ballpark" | A rough number |
| "A grand" | One thousand dollars |
| "Upside down" (on a home loan) | Owes more than the home is worth |
| "Behind on payments" | Missed loan or bill payments |

**Regional style (general, not stereotypes):**

- **Northeast:** often fast and direct. Get to the point quickly.
- **South:** often warmer and slower. "Sir" and "ma'am" are polite and common; small talk is welcome.
- **Midwest:** friendly and polite, may avoid saying "no" directly.
- **West:** often casual and relaxed.

**Titles:** with older customers, use "Mr." or "Ms." with their last name until they invite you to use their first name. "Sir" and "ma'am" are respectful, especially in the South. Use first names with clients in business settings.

**Small talk:** short and light is good. Safe topics: the weather where they are, the weekend, a holiday coming up. **Avoid:** politics, religion, guns, income, health, family problems, and jokes or sarcasm.

**Coach's note:** Check the weather in your customer's area before a calling block. "I hope you're staying warm, I saw it's snowing up there today" is honest, kind and shows you care. You are not pretending to be local, just paying attention.

## 5. Units and formats

| Topic | US uses |
| --- | --- |
| Temperature | Fahrenheit. 32 F is freezing, 70 F is comfortable, 100 F is very hot. |
| Distance | Miles (1 mile is about 1.6 km) |
| Home size | Square feet (1 square metre is about 10.8 square feet) |
| Land | Acres (1 acre is about 0.4 hectares) |
| Dates | Month first: 10/18/2026 means October 18, 2026 |
| Phone numbers | (405) 555-0134: area code, then 7 digits |
| Money | Dollars and cents; "K" for thousands (185K) |

## 6. Holidays

On US federal holidays many businesses are closed, people are with family and calling is often not welcome. **Narqix default: no outbound sales calls on US federal holidays**, unless your client's compliance team says otherwise.

| Holiday | When | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New Year's Day | January 1 |  |
| Martin Luther King Jr. Day | Third Monday of January |  |
| Presidents' Day | Third Monday of February |  |
| Memorial Day | Last Monday of May | Unofficial start of summer |
| Juneteenth | June 19 |  |
| Independence Day | July 4 | Fireworks, family events |
| Labor Day | First Monday of September | Unofficial end of summer |
| Columbus Day / Indigenous Peoples' Day | Second Monday of October | Some businesses stay open |
| Veterans Day | November 11 |  |
| Thanksgiving | Fourth Thursday of November | One of the biggest family holidays; many take Friday off too |
| Christmas Day | December 25 | Late December is quiet for most businesses |

**Other important days:** Easter Sunday (date changes each year; some states restrict calling on Sundays and holidays anyway), Good Friday and Mardi Gras (important in Louisiana), Mother's Day (second Sunday of May), Father's Day (third Sunday of June), Black Friday (day after Thanksgiving, busy shopping day), Super Bowl Sunday (early February, a huge sports evening).

**Holiday greetings:** "Happy holidays" is safe in December. Say "Happy Thanksgiving" or "Happy Fourth of July" around those dates if the customer seems friendly.

## 7. Seasons and weather events

- **Hurricane season:** June to November, mainly Florida, the Gulf Coast (Texas, Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama) and the Atlantic coast.
- **Tornado season:** mainly spring, in the central states (Oklahoma, Kansas, Texas, Nebraska and nearby).
- **Winter storms:** snow and ice in the North and Midwest, sometimes power cuts.
- **Wildfires:** summer and autumn, especially California and the West.
- **Heat waves:** summer in Texas, Arizona and the South. Important for HVAC and home services clients.

If a major disaster is in the news for your customer's area, be kind. Your campaign may pause calling there. Follow your lead's instructions, and never use a disaster as a sales angle.
